You framed overrides as giving people voice and letting revisions carry weight. She wrote that she would want responses to help see "what a recommendation may have missed about their needs."

That is not the same sentence back. It treats the override as diagnostic of an omission in the model, not only as preference data. Arguably a sharper framing than the one you handed her, and it is the version that actually generates research questions.

How to test it

Ask her what is wrong with your framing

Not a manufactured disagreement. A fair question between colleagues: what would she push back on in the way you described voice and choice, and where does she think it breaks down?

The honest answers exist. Whose voice counts when stakeholders conflict. What happens when an override is simply wrong. Whether transparency and solution quality trade off. If she has thought about the idea at all, she has an objection. If she produces none, that is the answer.

Better asked live than in writing, where she can compose.

Answering her, honestly

Include the unglamorous part

She asked what you expect at the outset. The truthful answer includes partner meetings, data that does not arrive clean, months before anything resembling a model, and very likely contributing to someone else's project before owning one.

Say that plainly. It is the second-best test you have, and unlike a clever question it costs you nothing to administer.

One caution. When describing partner projects, be accurate about what is yours to offer. The nonprofit resource sharing work is a multi-institution collaboration with partner organizations, not something your group hands out.
