Her scenario · first message

Hospital pharmacy under supply uncertainty

A pharmacy manager learns a critical drug may miss its promised date, with a few days of stock left. Act now and pay for a substitute, change treatment plans, or reserve doses for the highest-risk patients. Wait and keep the cost down, but lose options if the date slips again.

The system shows inventory, recent use, supplier estimate. It does not show which patients can safely switch, whether the supplier has been reliable, or how hard the substitution is for clinical staff. The record keeps the order, not why one signal was trusted over another.

My response

This is a problem statement, not a method looking for an application. She arrived at inverse optimization from here, not the other way round. Worth remembering when the mathematics starts to dominate the conversation again.

Her scenario · first message

Identification with incomplete observations

Recovering the tradeoffs behind institutional procurement decisions. Positive rescaling was the obvious ambiguity. The deeper difficulty is that unobserved demand and operational constraints let substantively different tradeoffs explain the same orders. Where observations cover a narrow part of the feasible set, some components are only partially identified.

She volunteered that the estimation procedure has not converged reliably and that she cannot yet tell whether the cause is implementation, conditioning, or non-identifiability.

My response

Said this plainly: I do not lead a program whose center is advancing that theory. Inverse optimization sits inside my work as a means. Reporting an unresolved failure rather than the tidy version is uncommon and I told her so.

This is the piece that would need a co-advisor or committee member if it stays central.

Her scenario · first message

Eliciting what a posted bid does not capture

In SWAP an organization posts an offer or places a bid, but the entry may not carry how urgently a resource is needed, whether staff have time to prepare or receive it, or whether the exchange is practical once transport and storage are counted.

Her proposal: find ways for organizations to supply that information without much extra work, then examine which signals actually predict whether a recommended exchange is accepted and completed. That evidence informs future clearing and routing while leaving organizations free to reject or revise.

My response

The strongest thing she wrote, and squarely in the interactive optimization space. Note she has not returned to it since. Her live project is the canteen study. Ask on the call whether this was aspirational or something she means to pursue.

If it becomes the direction, she needs to understand from day one that SWAP is a multi-institution collaboration with partner organizations, not a system my group owns.

Her scenario · latest message

Canteen company: who actually decides

A company operating canteens across multiple sites. Three years of procurement records.

  • Head chefs set menus and request ingredient types and quantities
  • A company manager coordinates purchasing and allocation but rarely changes those requests
  • Therefore quantities and ingredient selection encode chef preferences; supplier and delivery are central

My response

She mapped decision rights before modeling. Most students model first and find the org chart afterwards, usually too late. This is the single clearest signal of applied maturity in the whole exchange.

Her scenario · latest message

The data wall, named unprompted

  • No explicit penalty for overstocking, so managerial dissatisfaction is not an observed monetary cost
  • No independent measure of ingredient demand
  • Inventory records are month-end snapshots, so no stock on hand at the moment of each ordering decision, and no direct consumption or waste
  • Many records handwritten, ingredient names inconsistent, transcription unreliable, three years cleaned largely by hand

My response

She found the fatal problem herself and did not dress it up. The missing state variable at decision time is the one that sinks most projects of this shape.

It is also the first warning sign on scope. Three years of handwritten records cleaned by hand is a year of work that yields no paper on its own.

Her validation plan

Ask chefs and the manager to review recommendations for a small number of actual situations and explain whether they could implement them, what they would change, and why.

More generally, to understand the whole path from recommendation to implementation: who decides at each step, what practical constraints they face, what might prevent it being carried out, and to let that guide both the data collected and the model chosen.

My response

A face-validity protocol with the people who would have to act on it. This is my territory and the reason she identified me correctly. She has the instinct but has not yet lived the adoption problem.

Assessment

Where she genuinely needs mentoring

  • Scoping and stopping. What she describes could absorb five years. A dissertation needs three papers, not one heroic study.
  • Making a claim. Everything so far is descriptive and diagnostic. She has never said what the contribution is in a form someone could disagree with.
  • Whose preferences the model serves. Chefs want slack, the manager wants working capital freed. She describes the tension without seeing it as a modeling choice with stakes.
  • One site to a general result. N equals one company. Avoiding the consulting-report trap is a taught skill.
  • Deployment and adoption. She gestures at it correctly but has not done it.

On my own fit

Those five are my profile almost line for line. The one gap is the identification theory itself, which is what a committee member or co-advisor is for. A student fully covered by one advisor usually means the project is too small.
